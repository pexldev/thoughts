# Post-mortem — Shakti Aur Kshama, reel/short #3

Posted Aug 7 2026. Numbers read Aug 8.

| | Instagram Reel | YouTube Short |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | 60 views / 56 viewers | ~8 engaged views |
| Avg watch | **0:05** of 0:36 (14%) | **0:10** of 0:37 (27%) |
| Early exit | ~50% gone by 0:02 | **77.1% swiped away** |
| Shares / saves / comments | 0 / 0 / 0 | — |
| Likes | 1 (like rate 1.8%, *higher* than typical) | — |
| Follows | 1 (from 56 cold viewers) | — |
| Audience | **100% non-followers** | 94% mobile |
| Traffic | Reels tab 85%, Explore 13% | Shorts feed 92%, **search 5.6%** |

---

## What we learned

**1. A static text card cannot be frame one.** Replicated on two platforms with two different audiences. The reel opened on a yellow card with four lines of transliterated Hindi — to get anything from it you had to stop and *read eight words* before a single thing happened. That's not a hook, it's an entrance exam.

**2. Recognition hooks don't survive transliteration.** The theory was "Seedhe Maut fans will recognise their own lyric and stop." Wrong: that song lives in people's **ears**. Nobody recognises it in Latin script at scroll speed. A hook that requires decoding is not a hook.

**3. The material is fine. The entry was broken.** Once people were in, they behaved normally — like rate *higher* than his typical, skip rate *lower* than typical, YouTube marked watch duration "about the same as usual", and 1 of 56 cold viewers followed. Nothing about the writing or the delivery caused this.

**4. It holds twice as well on YouTube (0:10 vs 0:05).** Small sample, but consistent with the search-vs-feed logic: a feed scroller resents being asked to read, a searcher arriving on "shakti aur kshama" wants exactly that frame.

---

## What we missed

**1. We spent hours on minute three and zero minutes on second one.** Script, images, packaging, chapter system, posting order — all of it got real attention. The first two seconds, which decide whether any of the rest is ever seen, got none. That's the whole error in one sentence.

**2. We built a shortened episode, not a reel.** R3's structure was hook card → thesis → poem → payoff. That's essay architecture. Reel architecture is: give them something in second one, then earn second two, then three. We opened with ceremony.

**3. The image prompts were written as static compositions.** Nobody ever asked *"what MOVES in the first second?"* — the specs described what a frame looks like, never what happens in it.

**4. The "works on mute" bar passed something that failed.** A wall of legible text technically satisfies mute-legibility and still died. The bar was insufficient.

**5. YouTube title wasn't written for search.** It was published as the raw lyric, truncated. Search was already 5.6% of traffic on day one *with* a bad title — that was free upside left on the table. (Fixed after the fact; titles are editable.)

---

## Rules for the next one

1. **Write the first 3 seconds first**, as a separate creative task, before the rest of the cut exists.
2. **Frame one must contain a person, a face, or motion.** Text is a layer *over* something happening — never the thing itself.
3. **New mute bar: works on mute AND requires zero reading in the first 2 seconds.**
4. **Post the strongest cut first.** A reel that gets distribution teaches you something; one that doesn't teaches you nothing.
5. **YouTube titles are search assets.** Write them findable, not clever. Feed distribution is one-shot; search compounds for months.
6. **Judge by swipe-away rate, not views.** Views tell you how you feel. The exit point tells you what to fix.

---

## Open test

**R1 (Tyson) is the control.** It opens on an illustration with a person mid-action and a famous name. If swipe-away comes in meaningfully **under 77%**, the frame-one theory is confirmed and it becomes a standing rule. If it swipes away just as hard, the problem is bigger than the opening frame and we look again — at pacing, at length, at the cut itself.
