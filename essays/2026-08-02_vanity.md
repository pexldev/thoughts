## The source, and the actual subject

Bhagat Singh wrote that essay because a fellow prisoner accused him of **vanity** — that he'd stopped believing in God only because his own ego had swollen too big to fit anyone above him. The essay is a 23-year-old man, with a date of execution already set, defending his right to hold a conviction without being called arrogant for holding it.

**That is the essay I'm writing. Not atheism.** Religion is borrowed heat and I already ruled it out — the room reacts to the topic before my writing does any work. The portable thing is the charge itself.

**The connection:** on Aug 2 2026 I typed out my own education, my job, my bracket — and immediately stopped to ask whether it counted as boasting. Same charge. Ninety-six years apart. He was defending a conviction he was about to die for. I was defending saying out loud that I'm well read.

**Bhagat Singh's counter-move, worth stealing structurally:** he doesn't argue about God first. He argues about the *timeline* — he was an atheist **before** anyone knew his name, before the Assembly bombing made him famous. You think success made me arrogant; I was like this when I was nobody.

## What I actually think

**Q1. What is the accusation of vanity actually protecting?** Someone calls you arrogant for saying a true thing about yourself. Who benefits from that rule? What would happen to them if you stopped following it?

>

**Q2. Where do I enforce this rule on other people?** (Mandatory. No sermon without this.) When have I thought *who does he think he is* about someone doing exactly what I want to do?

>

**Q3. What's the difference between vanity and conviction — in behaviour, not in feeling?** Not "one feels humble." What does a vain man DO that a convinced man doesn't?

>

**Q4. What did the modesty actually cost me?** Concrete. A thing I didn't do, didn't say, didn't start, or hid for a year.

## The five beats

1. **THE SCENE.** Open with a thing that happened. Concrete, specific, small. A room, an object, a sentence someone said. **Never open with the thesis** — that's my default and it's what makes the opening flat. The idea is what I'm digging toward, not what I announce.
2. **THE ITCH.** Name the question the scene raised. Not the answer. *"Why did that bother me for three days?"*
3. **THE HONEST WALK.** Go looking. Take the obvious answer, then break it. Where's it true? Where's it false? What would the other person say back? This is the body, and it's supposed to wander a little — that's the *attempt*.
4. **THE TURN ONTO ME.** Non-negotiable. The point where I find that I'm guilty of the thing. This is what buys me the right to have an opinion at all — **no scar, no sermon.**
5. **THE LANDING.** Not advice. Not a moral. A changed way of seeing, or a question that's now sharper than it was at the top. Stop at the last strong sentence — anything after it is me explaining, and explanation kills essays the way it kills punchlines.
