# All these are strong ways of opening a loop


1. The Strong Opinion --> "I think self-improvement is one of the biggest scams on the internet."

    Bill Burr's favorite.

    "I think self-improvement is one of the biggest scams on the internet."

    Boom.

    Immediately I want to know why.

2. The Unexpected Confession --> "I spent three hours yesterday arguing with my refrigerator."

    Theo Von does this brilliantly.

    "I spent three hours yesterday arguing with my refrigerator."

    Obviously not literally.

    But I stay.

3. The Contradiction --> I think YouTube is more addictive than Instagram."

    My favorite.

    "I think YouTube is more addictive than Instagram."

    Instant curiosity.

    Because everyone expects the opposite.

4. The One Sentence Story --> "My uncle got a cornea transplant."

    Don't explain.

    Drop me in.

    Instead of

    "My uncle had surgery..."

    Start with

    "My uncle got a cornea transplant."

    Stop.

    That's interesting.

5. The Weird Question --> "Do rich people actually enjoy SUVs?"

    "Do rich people actually enjoy SUVs?"

    or

    "Why are CEOs buying cars they can't drive?"

    Questions activate the audience.

6. The Impossible Statement --> "We've made life so safe that we're afraid of everything."

    George Carlin loved these.

    "We've made life so safe that we're afraid of everything."

    You don't know if you agree.

    That's good.

7. The Visual Image --> "There are people falling asleep to a man explaining Roman tax laws."

    Instead of

    "People are addicted."

    Say

    "There are people falling asleep to a man explaining Roman tax laws."

    Now I see something.

8. The Self-own --> "I quit learning Spanish before I even learned Spanish."

    People love vulnerability.

    "I quit learning Spanish before I even learned Spanish."

    I'm listening.

9. Start at 95 MPH

    Don't warm up.

    Don't say

    "Hello everyone..."

    "Today's topic..."

    "Hope you're doing well..."

    Bill Burr is already angry.

    Theo Von is already weird.

    Andrew Santino is already arguing.

10. The Tiny Mystery

    "I think I accidentally discovered why adults can't sleep."

    Now I'm waiting.

    But here's something I've noticed about your writing.

    You naturally write openings like this:

    "Today I want to talk about..."

    or

    "I've been thinking..."

    or

    "A few weeks ago..."

    Those are slow ramps.

    They're not bad.

    They're just expensive.

On YouTube, you don't get a 30-second runway anymore.

Let me show you what I mean.

Your YouTube episode.

Instead of

"Today I want to talk about YouTube addiction."

Imagine

"I don't think Instagram is the problem anymore."

Pause.

"I think YouTube is."

Already better.

Your car episode.

Instead of

"As your salary increases..."

Imagine

"I think buying an SUV in Mumbai is one of the funniest financial decisions you can make."

I'm in.

Your fantasy episode.

Instead of explaining fantasy...

Start with

"I've quit things that I never even started."

That's an incredible first line.

Because it's impossible.

Then explain.

One technique that almost nobody talks about:
Start at the conclusion.

Movies do this.

Instead of

Problem →

Story →

Conclusion

Do

Conclusion →

Story →

Evidence

Example.

Instead of

"I downloaded Duolingo..."

Say

"I genuinely believe anxiety has killed more dreams than failure ever has."

Now prove it.

Another favorite of mine:
Make the audience disagree.

Not politically.

Intellectually.

Example.

"I don't think discipline changes your life."

Half the audience goes

"What??"

Perfect.

Now they have to stay.

Here's the formula I'd experiment with for your show.

Every opening should fit this:

Opinion + Curiosity + Personality

For example:

"I think we've completely misunderstood confidence."

Or:

"I think people don't actually want freedom."

Or:

"The most expensive thing I own isn't my car."

Or:

"I think YouTube is lying to us."

Notice how every one of those creates a question.

One final thing, bhai.

I think you've been optimizing for the first laugh.

I'd shift that slightly.

Optimize for the first lean-in.

Think about your own behavior on YouTube. The videos you keep watching often don't make you laugh in the first 10 seconds. They make you think:

"Where is this going?"

That feeling is more valuable than an early joke, because curiosity survives longer than a punchline.

If I were coaching you, I'd make you do one exercise for the next month:

Take every episode idea and write 10 different opening lines before writing the rest of the script. Don't judge them. Just keep asking, "Which one would make a stranger stop scrolling?"

I honestly think that single habit would improve your content more than writing 20 extra jokes per episode. That's because the opening determines whether anyone ever gets to hear those jokes.
