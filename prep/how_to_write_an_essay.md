# How to write an essay — the craft card
<!-- Built Aug 2 2026, the day I started the daily essay practice. Companion to how_to_talk_to_mic.md and how_to_structure_wednesday.md. -->

## First, the word

**"Essay" comes from the French *essai* — an ATTEMPT.** Montaigne invented the form in the 1500s and the whole idea was: I don't know the answer, watch me try to work it out on the page.

That's the definition to hold, because it kills my worst habit in one move:

> **If I know the ending before I start, I'm not writing an essay. I'm writing a lecture with paragraphs.**

An essay is a question I'm actually curious about, followed honestly until it turns into something I didn't expect. **If it didn't surprise me while writing it, it won't surprise the reader.** That's the whole test.

## The engine is the same as a joke

This is the part I already know how to do:

| Joke | Essay |
|---|---|
| Setup | The scene / the observation |
| Misdirection | The obvious take everyone would have |
| **Turn** | The thing I found that contradicts it |
| Tag | The escalation — one beat further |

**An essay is a long joke where the turn lands on the writer.** Same machine, longer runway. Everything in my /spar arsenal — who's the real victim, when is the opposite true, how am I the thing I'm describing — those are essay engines, not just joke engines.

## The five beats

1. **THE SCENE.** Open with a thing that happened. Concrete, specific, small. A room, an object, a sentence someone said. **Never open with the thesis** — that's my default and it's what makes the opening flat. The idea is what I'm digging toward, not what I announce.
2. **THE ITCH.** Name the question the scene raised. Not the answer. *"Why did that bother me for three days?"*
3. **THE HONEST WALK.** Go looking. Take the obvious answer, then break it. Where's it true? Where's it false? What would the other person say back? This is the body, and it's supposed to wander a little — that's the *attempt*.
4. **THE TURN ONTO ME.** Non-negotiable. The point where I find that I'm guilty of the thing. This is what buys me the right to have an opinion at all — **no scar, no sermon.**
5. **THE LANDING.** Not advice. Not a moral. A changed way of seeing, or a question that's now sharper than it was at the top. Stop at the last strong sentence — anything after it is me explaining, and explanation kills essays the way it kills punchlines.

## Orwell's six rules

He wrote these in *Politics and the English Language*, and they are the whole style guide:

1. Never use a metaphor, simile or figure of speech you're used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

**The operating principle underneath them:** never simplify the IDEA, always simplify the SENTENCE. Intellectual writers die of obscurity, not of intelligence. Carlin wasn't dumbing anything down — he was ruthlessly clear.

## The discipline

- **30–45 minutes, straight through, no stopping to fix.** Same law as the mic: never stop the tape. Editing while generating is what kills both.
- **800–1,200 words.** Long enough to turn, short enough to finish daily.
- **Write to ONE person.** Same fix as the mic — a reader is a void, a friend is a person.
- **Read it out loud before it's done.** Every sentence I stumble on is badly written, not badly read. This doubles as the mic drill.

## The four checks (my tells)

- [ ] Opens with a **thing**, not a thesis
- [ ] It **surprised me** somewhere in the middle — if not, I wrote a lecture
- [ ] The sermon is **paid for** by self-implication
- [ ] No "I felt something" — behaviour and objects instead
