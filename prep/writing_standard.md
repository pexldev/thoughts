## The line

> # "I write for one person in headphones. No crowd carries me — every laugh is earned in the sentence."

**The 12-month constraint (my call, 15 Aug 2026):** no stage in the plan for a year.

Picture one actual person: on the metro, earphones in, going to work. That's who I'm writing for. Not a hall. **One person, alone.**

---

## What that person changes about the writing

**1. Nobody laughs first.**
In a club, one person laughs and everyone follows — laughing is catching. My line only has to be okay and the room does the rest. On the metro, nobody starts it. If the line isn't funny on its own, it just sits there. **The line has to work with zero help.**

**2. I can't use my face.**
On stage I can pause, pull a face, shrug, and get a laugh without saying anything. They can't see me. Everything is in the words and the voice.

**3. Nobody claps on the metro.**
Say a big true thing on stage and the crowd applauds. In earphones they go "hm, true" and keep walking. **Big true things get me nothing here. Only jokes work.**

**4. "Hello everybody" sounds insane in someone's ear.**
Crowd energy — hyping, shouting, "how we doing tonight" — is fake when it's one person and I'm two inches from their ear. Sound like I'm talking to a friend, quietly.

**5. They're not really listening.**
Dishes, walking, half thinking about work. Attention drifts about every minute. So grabbing them at the start isn't enough — **grab them again, every minute, all the way through.**

**6. Their thumb is right there.**
No ticket, no usher, nothing holding them. Boring first 20 seconds = swipe. **The first 20 seconds matter more than anything else in the episode.**

They can also rewind, so density is safe — I can go fast. Under-writing is the bigger risk than over-writing.

---

## Why hard mode is the right mode (settled 15 Aug)

My argument, and it holds: **if I can crack the person listening alone, the room is not an uphill climb afterwards** — a room only ever *adds* help. Material built with no crutches is over-engineered for a stage. That's the half that takes years, and I'm doing it first.

Bonus nobody talks about: with no laughs to tell me, I have to learn to **know** it's funny without being told. Club comics often never build that — they let the room tell them. I'm building the judgment first, and that can't be bought later.

**What will NOT transfer, so I don't kid myself:** the performing. Riding a laugh (waiting for the peak, coming back in on the drop), saving a dying bit live, hecklers, nerves in front of actual humans. And the sharp one — **the rhythm I'm training is wrong for a stage.** In headphones I fill everything, silence is dead air; on stage I have to leave gaps for laughs. I'd have to unlearn the pace.

> **Verdict: years of advantage, months of catch-up. Good trade.** Not "easy" — *fast*, on top of an unfair advantage.

**The cost of hard mode:** no feedback, so I learn slower. I can spend a month on a bit and never find out it didn't land. That's exactly why the three signals exist — shares, the cold tape a week later, one human before recording. **Hard mode with no signal is just guessing.**

## THE STANDARD — one rule, both formats

> # Every minute pays in a laugh or in blood.
> # A minute that pays in neither is a TED talk.

A TED talk pays in **insight**, which costs the speaker nothing. That's why it can be interesting and still unbearable. Laughs and confession are the only two currencies where I actually spend something.

The formats are not different rules. They're **different mixes of the same two currencies.**

| | **JOKE LAB** | **WEDNESDAY** |
|---|---|---|
| The unit | An **opinion** with jokes riding it | A **confession** with an argument inside it |
| Pays mostly in | Laughs | Blood |
| Hard floor | **≥1 blood payment per episode** (the self-turn) | **Never fewer than one laugh per minute** — below that it's a diary |
| Runtime | 10–15 min *(the only hard constraint)* | As long as the confession earns |
| Fires | Weekly, Friday | Only when something actually happened — event-driven *(paused as a weekly since 18 Jul; this is the spec for when a one-off fires)* |
| Fails as | A list of jokes on a topic | A therapy session nobody asked to attend |

**Why "opinion" and not "thesis":** a thesis wants **agreement**, and agreement gets applause. An opinion wants **recognition**, and recognition gets the laugh. That is the entire difference between a bit and a talk — the same disease as sermon-closes, wearing a different coat.

---

## JOKE LAB — the spec

**It is:** 1–3 observations per episode, each built as a **threaded bit** — a run of jokes riding ONE shared opinion. Not two-liners bang-bang (retired 18 Jul). Not a topic with jokes stapled to it. One episode can be a single observation if it earns 10–15 min (ep5 did, at 8–9).

**What goes in it:**
- A saying every Indian repeats, taken apart — the house format. Or a shared observation.
- **The test: has the listener also done this?** If it's only my life, it's a Wednesday, not a Joke Lab.
- Anecdotes may be invented. **The thesis has to be true; the story doesn't.**

**How it's built:**
- The glue is the shared **opinion**, so bridges can be one breath: *"and the other thing about this line…"*
- Jokes escalate. A tag on one joke can BE the setup of the next.
- One callback planted early, detonated at the end.
- **3–4 informal break-character asides per observation** — *"ohh that's a bad one"*, *"my dad will kill me"*. A script with zero asides records flat. That's a lab note bought with a bad episode.
- Bridges and asides are **my** mouth-words, written at assembly.

**Density bar** *(target to calibrate against the ep5 baseline, not law yet)*:
- Something breaks **at least every 30 seconds**; average one every **20**.
- **Twenty seconds to the first break.** Carlin gets a minute of runway before his first laugh because thirty years bought that trust. I haven't bought it. **Earn the runway, don't assume it.**
- **Every observation ends on a break, never on a position.**

---

## WEDNESDAY — the spec

**It is:** a confession with an argument inside it. The turn onto me is the point; the idea is what the confession pays for.

**What goes in it — the trigger test:** *would I tell this to a friend, unprompted, this week?* If no, it isn't a Wednesday. This format doesn't run on schedule, it runs on events.

**How it's built:**
- Opens on a **thing** — a room, an object, a sentence someone said. Never on the thesis.
- The confession arrives **fast**. Bobby Lee's speed — no runway, no hedge. My documented tell is hedging into *"I felt something"* exactly at the embarrassing part; behaviour and objects instead.
- The sermon is allowed **only after it's paid for.** Sermon, then the price. (The Shooting in the Dark close is the working model: noble line, then the petty revenge that reveals the real motive.)
- **I write every line.** Claude does structure, diagnosis, cuts, pressure-testing — never the lines.

## The pass/fail checklist — run before recording anything

- [ ] Every minute pays in a **laugh or in blood**. Name which, minute by minute.
- [ ] First break inside **20 seconds**.
- [ ] Nothing goes **30 seconds** without a break.
- [ ] Every section **ends on a break, not a position**. *(Could they clap here instead of laugh? Then it's a setup, not an ending.)*
- [ ] At least one thing in here **costs me something**.
- [ ] Setups are **one sentence**.
- [ ] Punch word **last**, every time.
- [ ] None of the four gap-fillers: explaining, stated emotion, announcing, words after the punch.
- [ ] The **15% cut pass** has been run.
- [ ] Read out loud once, timed.
