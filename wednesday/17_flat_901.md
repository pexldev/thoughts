# Wednesday Episode 17: Flat 901
<!-- Working title for /packaging: "I Know Everything About Her Marriage. We've Never Spoken." -->

**The incident (one line):** Every weekend I hear the couple one floor below me fight, and her cry, through my bedroom floor — and every weekend I almost knock, and don't.
**The antagonist:** Nobody speaks AGAINST me — the house help speaks (the verdict), the couple speaks (through the floor), and the loudest voice is my own brain drafting knock-speeches.
**The one question I actually can't answer:** If you were her — would you want the guy upstairs to knock? Or never, ever knock?

---

## RECORDING SCRIPT

### 1. COLD OPEN *(0:00–0:15)*

I think her husband cheated on her.

I think she caught him.

I think he even admitted it.

I have never spoken to either of these people.

I don't know their names.

### 2. REWIND *(0:15–0:45)*

Okay. Let me rewind.

There's a couple who lives one floor below me.

I've changed the flat numbers for privacy.

If my flat is 1001...

hers is 901.

If you crack this code, honestly, you deserve to find them.

Now — the builder sold us "premium living."

I've found the premium feature.

The floors are so thin, I get someone else's marriage... included in my maintenance.

### 3. THE SCENE *(0:45–2:30)*

Almost every weekend, they fight.

Not the normal husband-wife argument. You know the normal one — few loud voices, then silence, then someone's watching TV like nothing happened.

This is different.

The fight ends...

and a few minutes later, she goes into the bathroom attached to their bedroom...

and cries.

Not crying.

Weeping.

The kind of crying where you don't need to know the person...

your body just goes — something is terribly wrong down there.

⭐ **SHORT #1 starts here (the house-help verdict scene)**

So I did what any mature adult would do.

I launched an investigation.

Our house help works in their flat too.

And I asked her about them — casually.

You know the casual voice that has forty-five minutes of preparation behind it?

"Waise... 901 waale... kaise log hain?"

She didn't even stop mopping.

"Didi bahut achi hai.

Bhaiyya bewda hai."

Two sentences. Case closed.

I have three months of audio evidence and no conclusion.

She has zero evidence and a full verdict.

That's the difference between me and my house help.

I collect data.

She has wisdom.

⭐ **SHORT #1 ends here**

### 4. ESCALATION — three waves *(2:30–4:30)*

**Wave 1 — the series**

Because here's the thing about living in an apartment.

You accidentally become part of other people's lives.

I never chose to know this. The floor just... kept sending updates.

And over three months, from random sentences through concrete...

my brain has stitched together an entire Netflix series.

Season one: the fight.

Season two: the confession.

Season three: he asks for a divorce.

I want to be clear — I'm not proud of this.

I didn't sit there with a notebook.

My brain did this without my permission.

Some people hear sounds at night and think "rats."

My brain heard sounds and produced a limited series.

I'm a consultant. I make dashboards for a living.

Apparently my brain now does it off the clock. For free. For a marriage I'm not in.

**Wave 2 — the update (the ugly confession, small)**

⭐ **SHORT #2 candidate starts here**

And it got worse.

One Saturday night, 11 PM, I hear their door slam.

And some disgusting little part of my brain went —

"New episode."

I hate that guy.

I evicted him immediately. He's back every Saturday.

⭐ **SHORT #2 candidate ends here**

**Wave 3 — the knock (peak)**

Because late at night, sometimes, I hear her on the phone.

Calling him.

He's not picking up. Or he's not coming home. I don't know.

And every single time, I have the exact same thought.

Should I just go downstairs...

knock on the door...

and say — "Hey. Are you okay?"

"Do you want to come upstairs? I've got friends over. Just sit with us for a while."

Because from whatever little I've seen...

I don't think she has many people.

She seems... alone.

⭐ **SHORT #3 starts here (the knock rehearsal act-out)**

And I have rehearsed this knock.

Multiple drafts.

Draft one: "Hi, you don't know me, I live upstairs, and I've noticed that on Thursdays he doesn't—"

Police.

Draft two: "Hi, I couldn't help but overhear the last three months of your—"

Police.

Draft three: I say nothing. I just stand there holding a bowl of Maggi like a UN peace offering.

Police. And this time, honestly? Correct decision.

There is no version of "I've been listening to your marriage" that sounds kind.

I know. I've drafted six.

Every draft starts as kindness and ends as the first five minutes of a crime documentary.

⭐ **SHORT #3 ends here**

And say the impossible happens. Say she comes upstairs.

Then what?

She's on my sofa.

My friends are dealing her into UNO.

Her husband hasn't come home in two days...

and a stranger she met four minutes ago looks her in the eye and goes —

"Plus four."

And me — the host — what do I offer her?

"Do you want a drink?"

No. Wait.

That's the exact problem downstairs.

So I'm just... making chai for a woman whose crying I can identify through concrete...

whose name I do not know.

### 5. THE SYSTEM *(4:30–5:30)*

And that's the thing nobody tells you about apartments.

You think you're buying a flat.

You're actually subscribing to the two families directly above and below you.

No trailer. No consent screen. Auto-renewal.

In my grandfather's village, everyone knew everything about everyone — that was the deal, that was the plan.

The city fixed it.

Now you know nothing about anyone...

except the two flats your ceiling and floor are touching.

Full intimacy. Zero relationship.

I know how 901 cries.

901 knows... whatever I am at 2 AM.

We've never made eye contact in the lift.

### 6. THE SELF-TURN *(5:30–6:30)*

And somewhere in month two, I caught my own brain doing the judge thing.

"If it's this bad — why doesn't she just leave?

Even if divorce is complicated — live separately. Figure it out."

Bro.

You have been rehearsing a KNOCK for three months.

You have six drafts of one sentence.

She's supposed to restart her entire life...

and you can't walk down one floor of stairs.

And here's the truth I don't like saying out loud.

I tell myself I don't knock because it would scare her.

That's not it.

I don't knock because it would be awkward for me.

Her loneliness, apparently, I can live with.

My awkwardness — impossible. Cannot risk it.

Even bhaiyya probably has a version of this story where he makes sense.

I know he does.

I've heard him tell it. Saturday nights. Loudly.

And I have a version where I make sense too.

I'm "respecting her privacy."

Very convenient, no? My cowardice and my manners look exactly the same.

### 7. THE FLIP *(6:30–7:00)*

And look at the irony of it all.

There's a woman in my building I know everything about.

She doesn't know that I know.

She's down there thinking nobody in the world sees her.

And I'm up here... recording a podcast episode about her.

But here's the part that actually keeps me up.

The floor works both ways.

The same concrete that leaks her marriage up to me...

leaks me down to her.

I've never tested this. But physics doesn't take sides.

If I can hear her whisper...

she can hear me podcast.

Which means she has heard a grown man say the same joke eleven times... alone... into a microphone.

By the time you hear this episode —

she already heard it.

Through the ceiling.

First listener.

For all I know, she's lying there every weekend thinking...

"The poor guy upstairs. Talking to himself again.

Somebody should knock on HIS door."

### 8. THE CLOSE *(last 45s)*

I could be completely wrong about all of it.

I'm hearing one apartment through a floor. Maybe I built a whole series out of nothing.

But the crying is real. That part I'm sure of.

So here's my question — and I actually don't have the answer.

If you were her.

Would you want the guy upstairs to knock?

Or never, ever knock?

Because I've been standing at my door for three months.

If you saw something in this I didn't — tell me in the comments.

Because... I could be wrong.

My name is Prateek.

I'll see you in the next one.
